Why creative directors become accidental project managers

When nobody clearly owns the chasing, scheduling and client wrangling, it drifts to your most senior creatives, and creative leadership pays the price.

By Christina Lawson · 5 min read

Ask most creative directors how their week went and you'll hear a lot about things that aren't creative direction. Chasing a client for feedback. Reshuffling the schedule because a freelancer dropped out. Answering "is this approved?" for the fourth time.

Nobody hired them to do this. They just ended up doing it, one small job at a time.

I call this operational responsibility creep, and it's one of the most common patterns in independent agencies. It's rarely anyone's fault. It's usually a gap that the most capable person in the room quietly filled.

How it happens

Picture a typical Tuesday. A client emails the creative director directly, because that's who they met at the pitch. A designer asks whether a deadline can move. The account lead is out, so the CD answers a scope question too. Each thing takes five minutes. None of it feels like a big deal.

Senior creatives absorb this work for understandable reasons. They care about the outcome, they have the context and they're the person everyone already trusts. If nobody else clearly owns chasing, scheduling or client wrangling, it drifts towards whoever is most likely to handle it well.

Over a few months, those five-minute jobs add up to a second role. One that never appeared in anyone's job description.

What it costs

The obvious cost is time. Every hour spent chasing feedback is an hour not spent reviewing work, developing people or thinking about where the studio's creative is heading.

The less obvious cost is focus. Creative leadership needs long, uninterrupted stretches. It's hard to give a team thoughtful direction in between messages about invoice dates and courier bookings.

Then there's morale, on both sides. The creative director feels stretched and, if they're honest, a bit resentful. Studio and project managers feel undermined, because decisions they should be making keep getting made over their heads. And designers learn that the fastest route to an answer is to skip the process and go straight to the top, which only feeds the cycle.

When the creative director is also the scheduler, the escalation point and the client's favourite inbox, the creative work is usually the thing that gives.

The real issue is unclear roles

Most of the time, this isn't a workload problem. It's a clarity problem.

In plenty of studios, nobody has ever written down who decides what. Can the studio manager move a deadline without checking? Who agrees to a change in scope? Who tells the client "no"? When those answers are fuzzy, people default to asking the most senior person nearby, just to be safe.

I spent years in roles that sat right on this line, first running an in-house ecommerce studio and later as a creative project manager. The difference between a calm week and a chaotic one was rarely the amount of work. It was whether everyone knew which decisions were theirs to make.

How to fix it

You don't need a restructure. You need a few clear conversations, and the discipline to stick to what you agree.

  1. Map who owns what. List the recurring operational tasks: chasing feedback, booking freelancers, scheduling, scope changes, client updates. Next to each, write one name. If the answer is "it depends" or "the CD, I suppose", you've found your gap.
  2. Separate creative and operational decisions. Creative decisions (is this the right idea, is the work good enough) belong with creative leadership. Operational ones (when, who, how much) belong with whoever runs the studio. Say this out loud, so the whole team hears it.
  3. Give studio and project managers real authority. If they have to check every call with the CD, they're not managing, they're relaying. Agree what they can decide on their own, then back them when they do.
  4. Protect leadership time. Block regular time for creative review and team development, and treat it like a client meeting. Redirect operational questions to the right owner, kindly and consistently.

That last one is often the hardest, because it asks the creative director to stop being helpful in the old way. It takes practice. It also takes a team that trusts the new owners to pick things up.

Try this

  • For one week, note every non-creative task your CD handles, and who should have owned it.
  • Write a one-page list of decisions your studio manager can make without checking with anyone.
  • Ask clients to send timeline and admin questions to a named project contact rather than the CD.
  • Book two protected creative review sessions into your CD's diary for next week.

Creative directors become project managers by accident, so it makes sense to fix it on purpose. A little clarity about who owns what gives your senior creatives their real job back, and gives your operational people room to do theirs.
