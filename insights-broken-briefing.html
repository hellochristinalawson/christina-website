How to spot a broken briefing process

The warning signs that your briefs are costing you time, and the simple fixes that get work moving in the right direction from day one.

By Christina Lawson · 5 min read

Picture the kickoff. Everyone's in the room, ready to go. Twenty minutes in, someone asks who the audience actually is, and the room goes quiet.

Most agencies don't have a creative problem. They have a briefing problem that shows up later as a creative problem. The work goes in the wrong direction, the team gets blamed for missing the mark, and nobody looks back at where it really started.

Here's how to tell if your briefing process is broken, why it happens, and what to do about it.

The warning signs

A broken briefing process rarely announces itself. It leaks out in small, familiar ways. If a few of these sound like your studio, it's worth paying attention.

  • Brief_v7_FINAL_updated.docx. If your briefs go through seven versions, the thinking is happening inside the document instead of before it.
  • Kickoffs that turn into briefing sessions. The kickoff should be about how you'll do the work. If it's spent working out what the work is, the brief wasn't ready.
  • Work going back and forth. Round after round of amends, each one nudging the work somewhere slightly different.
  • "That's not what we asked for." The most expensive sentence in any studio. It usually means nobody agreed what was asked for in the first place.
  • Briefs living in email threads. The real brief is scattered across a forwarded chain, a call someone half remembers and a comment in a shared deck.

None of these on their own means disaster. Together, they mean your team is spending creative energy on guesswork.

Why it happens

In my experience, briefing rarely breaks because people are careless. It breaks because nobody designed it. It just grew.

There's no template. Every account lead writes briefs their own way. Some are three lines in an email, some are twelve-page decks. The studio never knows what it's going to get, so it learns to expect gaps.

There's no owner. When everyone contributes to a brief, nobody is responsible for it being complete. Questions float around until a designer has to chase them.

Things get lost between sales and studio. The person who won the work had great conversations with the client. Very little of that makes it across. The context, the politics, the thing the client said they hated last time, all of it stays in someone's head.

Sign-off is unclear. If you don't know who approves the brief, you don't really have one. You have a starting point that anyone can change.

The fixes

The good news is that briefing is one of the easiest things to improve. You don't need new software. You need a few agreed habits.

A one-page brief template

Keep it to a single page. If it's longer, people skip sections. Cover the essentials: the objective, the audience, the key message, deliverables, mandatories, budget, timings and who approves. Leave space for "what we know we don't know yet", because honest gaps are better than hidden ones.

Put it wherever your team already works, whether that's a shared doc, a project tool or a form. The format matters far less than everyone using the same one.

A brief sign-off step

Before anything goes into the studio, the brief gets signed off. By the client, by the account lead and by whoever will lead the creative. It takes ten minutes and saves days.

This is also where the studio gets to push back. If the creative lead reads it and has five questions, it isn't ready yet. That's the system working, not slowing you down.

A short kickoff checklist

With a solid brief, kickoffs can focus on doing the work. A simple checklist keeps them on track:

  • Has everyone read the brief before the meeting?
  • Are there any open questions, and who's chasing them?
  • What are the key dates and review points?
  • Who's doing what?

If the kickoff runs over, take note of why. It's usually pointing at something missing from the brief.

Agree who approves

Name one person on the client side who signs off the work, and one person internally who signs it off before it goes out. Write their names on the brief. When feedback arrives from someone else, you'll know how to handle it, and you'll have something to point to.

Try this

  • Pull up your last three briefs and count how many versions each went through.
  • Ask your designers what they wish every brief told them. Write it down.
  • Draft a one-page template from those answers and test it on your next new project.
  • Add a "who approves" line to every brief going forward, starting today.
  • At your next kickoff, note how much time goes on clarifying the brief.

A good brief won't guarantee great work. But a broken one will make great work much harder than it needs to be. Fix the briefing and you give your team the best possible start.
