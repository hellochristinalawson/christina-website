The processes that got you to 10 people won't necessarily get you to 30

The informal habits that make a small studio feel fast start to creak as you grow, and a few clear agreements will do more than any big overhaul.

By Christina Lawson · 5 min read

At ten people, most agencies run on something I'd call "just ask". Need the latest brand guidelines? Just ask. Not sure who's on the retainer account this week? Just ask. It works, because everyone sits within shouting distance and the founder knows where everything is.

Then you hire your eleventh person, and your fifteenth, and somewhere around twenty the shouting distance stops covering the room. Nothing dramatic happens. Things just get a little slower, a little messier and a little more tiring.

That isn't a sign you've done something wrong. It's a sign the habits that got you here were built for a smaller version of your studio.

Why "just ask" works, until it doesn't

Informal systems are brilliant when a team is small. They're fast, they're flexible and they cost nothing to run. Nobody needs a brief template when the brief is a five-minute chat over coffee.

The trouble is that "just ask" only scales as far as the number of people you can ask. Every new hire adds more conversations, more handovers and more chances for something to fall between two people who each assumed the other had it.

I've seen versions of this in both in-house and agency teams. When I was resourcing designers and freelancers on global brand accounts, the work that ran smoothly was rarely the work with the cleverest people on it. It was the work where everyone knew where to look and who to go to.

The manual that lives in the founder's head

In most growing agencies, the most important operating manual isn't written down anywhere. It lives in the founder's head. Which client hates yellow. Which freelancer is great at motion but needs a tight brief. Why the retainer is scoped the way it is.

At ten people, that was fine, because you were in every conversation. At twenty-five, you can't be, so the team ends up either guessing or waiting for you.

Neither is great. Guessing creates rework. Waiting creates a queue, and the queue has your name on it.

If you're the only person who knows how things work, you've become the system. And systems that need sleep don't scale well.

Decisions follow the same pattern. When every approval, priority call and "quick one" has to go through you, the studio can only move as fast as your inbox. Your team isn't short of talent. They're short of permission and information.

The first signs you've outgrown things

The early signals are usually small and easy to explain away. Watch for:

  • Missed handovers. Work stalls between people because nobody was clear who picked it up next.
  • Duplicated work. Two designers quietly working on the same thing, or a deck being rebuilt because nobody could find the last version.
  • The founder as router. Every question lands with you, including plenty you shouldn't need to see.
  • Status meetings that keep growing. You add check-ins to feel in control, and they eat the time people need to do the work.
  • Slow onboarding. If settling in means "shadow someone for a month", the knowledge isn't anywhere a new person can find it.

If two or three of those sound familiar, you're not imagining it.

What to formalise first

The instinct, once things feel chaotic, is to go big. A new project management tool, a forty-page process handbook, another weekly meeting. I'd gently suggest not doing that. Most agencies don't need more process. They need a few clear agreements in the places where things currently leak.

I usually start with three:

  1. Intake. One way for work to come into the studio. Not five Slack channels, two inboxes and a corridor chat. A simple request form in a tool you already use is plenty.
  2. Ownership. Every live job has one named owner who's responsible for moving it forward. Not necessarily the person doing the work, but the person who makes sure it doesn't stall.
  3. A single source of truth. One place where anyone can check what's happening, who's on it and when it's due. If people still have to ask to find out, it isn't working yet.

Get those three right and a lot of the "just ask" traffic starts to fade on its own.

Keeping it light

Formalising doesn't have to mean bureaucracy. The test I use is simple: does this make someone's day easier, or does it just make the process look tidier? If it's the second one, cut it.

Write things down in the plainest way you can. Introduce one change at a time and give it a few weeks before you judge it. And ask your team where they lose time, because they usually know better than anyone.

Try this

  • Keep a tally of every question that lands with you in one day, and mark the ones someone else could have answered.
  • Pick one live project and ask three people who owns it. See whether the answers match.
  • List every route new work currently arrives by, then choose one to make the official route.
  • Write down three things only you know about your biggest client, and put them somewhere the team can find them.

Growing past ten people is a good problem to have. It just needs a slightly different set of habits from the ones that got you started. If you'd like a second pair of eyes on where things are leaking, that's exactly what a Workflow Audit is for.
