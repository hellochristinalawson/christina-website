The hidden cost of “just one quick change”

Small requests feel harmless one at a time, but together they quietly eat into your team's capacity and your margins.

By Christina Lawson · 5 min read

“Can we just make the logo a bit bigger?” “Could you try it in blue?” “One quick tweak and we're done, promise.”

Every creative team has heard these. On their own, each one sounds tiny. Five minutes, maybe ten. Saying no would feel petty.

But quick changes rarely stay quick, and they rarely come alone. Left unmanaged, they become one of the biggest hidden drains on a studio's time.

How quick changes add up

Say a client asks for a headline tweak on a social campaign. Simple enough. Except the headline appears across twelve sizes, two of which now need the layout adjusting. Then the account lead spots that the CTA no longer lines up. Then it goes back to the client, who now wants to see the original next to the new one.

That “five-minute” change has become half a day, spread across three people.

Multiply that across every client and every week, and you can see where the time goes. It's rarely one big request that breaks a schedule. It's dozens of small ones nobody planned for.

The real cost is the interruption

The time spent making the change is only part of it. The bigger cost is what it interrupts.

A designer deep in a new identity project gets a Slack message about a quick fix on something from last month. They stop, find the files, remember where they left things, make the change, export, send, and then try to get back into the work they were doing. Getting back into creative flow takes time, and it doesn't show up on any timesheet.

It's rarely one big request that breaks a schedule. It's dozens of small ones nobody planned for.

Do that a few times a day and your team ends up feeling busy all day while making slow progress on the work that matters most.

Scope creep or relationship worries?

Most agency teams know when something is out of scope. The hesitation is usually about the relationship. Nobody wants to be the agency that charges for moving a comma, especially with a client you value.

That's a fair instinct. But absorbing every change quietly isn't really protecting the relationship. It's teaching the client that changes are free and unlimited, and it's putting the cost onto your team instead.

The good news is there's a middle ground between “sure, no problem” and “that'll be extra”. It starts with being clear up front.

Simple routines that protect your team

None of this needs a complicated system. It needs a few agreed habits, set early and used consistently.

  • Agree revision rounds at the start. Put the number of feedback rounds in the proposal or brief. Two is common. Everyone then knows what's included before work begins.
  • Ask for consolidated feedback. One set of comments, from one person, by an agreed date. Scattered feedback from five stakeholders across email and chat is where a lot of rework starts.
  • Route changes through one person. Requests go to the account or project lead, not straight to a designer. That person decides whether it's a quick fix, part of a round, or something new.
  • Use a simple change-request routine. For anything outside the agreed scope, note what's being asked, what it affects, and whether it changes the timeline or cost. A short shared document or a template email is plenty.
  • Batch small fixes. Rather than handling tweaks as they arrive, collect them and schedule a set time to work through them.

Say “yes, and here's what it affects”

This is my favourite habit to introduce, because it keeps things friendly. You don't need to say no. You say yes, and you're honest about the knock-on effect.

“Yes, we can switch the colourway. It'll mean updating all twelve formats, so delivery moves from Thursday to Monday. Happy to go ahead?”

Most clients appreciate that. It treats them as a partner, helps them prioritise, and often they'll decide the change isn't worth the delay after all. Either way, the decision is now theirs to make with full information, rather than your team's to quietly absorb.

Try this

  • Ask your team to log every “quick change” they handle this week, with a rough time against each one.
  • Check your current proposals and briefs. Do they say how many revision rounds are included?
  • Write a short template reply for out-of-scope requests that uses “yes, and here's what it affects”.
  • Agree who on your team receives change requests, and let your designers know they can redirect them.
  • Pick one client and ask for consolidated feedback on the next round.

Quick changes will never disappear entirely, and they shouldn't. Being responsive is part of good client service. The aim is simply to make the cost visible, so your team isn't paying for it in late nights and lost focus.
