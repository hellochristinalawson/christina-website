Insights · Resourcing

Your creative team isn't at capacity. Your resource planning is.

When everyone is busy but no one is free for the next project, the problem usually isn't the team. It's the plan.

By Christina Lawson · 5 min read

A new brief lands. It's a good one. You look at the team and everyone is flat out, so you start wondering whether to hire, bring in a freelancer, or push the start date back a fortnight.

Then you look at the resourcing sheet, and on paper there's room. Two designers show half-empty weeks. So where did all that time go?

In my experience, the team usually isn't the problem. The plan is. It's measuring the wrong things, so it can't tell you what you actually need to know: who can take on what, and when.

The work that never makes it onto the plan

Most resource plans track projects. Very few track everything else a creative person does in a day.

Picture a typical Tuesday for a mid-weight designer. A status meeting. Two rounds of feedback on last week's work. A quick resize someone asked for on Slack. Hunting for the latest logo files. Answering a question from a freelancer. None of it is on the plan, and all of it takes time.

  • Admin: timesheets, file prep, chasing assets, updating trackers.
  • Feedback rounds: often planned as one round when three is closer to normal.
  • Context switching: jumping between four projects means a little time lost every time someone changes gear.

Add that up across a week and a "40-hour" designer might have far fewer hours for the project work you've booked them on. The plan says they're free. They feel anything but.

Bookings aren't reality

Here's the other quiet problem. Most plans show what someone is booked to do, not what they're actually doing.

A project gets booked for three days in week one. It slips, because the client is late with copy. Now it's sitting on top of week two, alongside everything else that was already there. The plan still shows week one as busy and week two as fine.

Optimistic estimates make this worse. Under pressure to win work or hit a client's date, it's natural to scope a job at the best-case number. Nobody puts "plus two days of waiting for sign-off" in the estimate, even though everyone knows it'll happen.

A plan that only shows bookings tells you what you hoped would happen, not what's happening.

No one can see the whole picture

In a lot of independent agencies, capacity lives in several places at once. The studio manager has a spreadsheet. An account lead has their own view of their clients. The creative director knows who's struggling because they sat next to them yesterday. Freelancers are tracked in someone's inbox.

Each view is partly right. None of them is the full picture. So when a new project comes in, the question "can we take this on?" gets answered by whoever is in the room, based on whichever view they happen to hold.

When I was running an in-house studio, and later managing resourcing for a large pool of designers and freelancers, the biggest shift was simply getting everyone looking at the same thing. Not a fancier tool. One shared, honest view.

Simple fixes that make a real difference

You don't need new software or a complicated framework. You need a few habits that make the plan reflect real life.

  1. Check capacity before you commit dates. Make it a rule that no start date goes to a client until someone has looked at the plan. A quick weekly capacity check is enough for most teams.
  2. Plan for real hours, not contracted hours. If a designer realistically has around five or six hours a day for project work, plan with that number. The rest is not wasted. It's the meetings, admin and feedback that keep things moving.
  3. Make unplanned work visible. Create a simple line on the plan for ad hoc requests and quick fixes. You'll quickly see how much space they really take up, and where they're coming from.
  4. Give the plan one owner. Everyone can contribute, but one person keeps it up to date and has the final say on whether something fits. Without that, the plan drifts until nobody trusts it.
  5. Add the waiting time to estimates. Build in time for feedback rounds and client delays as standard, rather than treating them as surprises.

Try this

  • Ask each person on your team to jot down everything they did on one day this week, including the small stuff. Compare it to the plan.
  • Pick a time for a 20-minute capacity check next Monday, before any new dates go out.
  • Reduce each person's plannable hours to a realistic number and see what the next fortnight looks like.
  • Add an "unplanned work" row to your resourcing sheet and fill it in for a week.
  • Decide who owns the plan, and tell the team.

When the plan reflects how your team actually works, the answer to "can we take this on?" gets much clearer. You stop guessing, your team stops absorbing the gap, and you can make hiring decisions based on real numbers rather than a general feeling of overwhelm.
